Methodology — Read This First

The ETFs themselves do not have 20-year history. Proxy indices were used for the backtest period (2007–2026):



Line Proxy                                            Note
VWRP MSCI World (MXWO)                                Developed markets only — slightly understates EM exposure
VAGS Bloomberg Global Aggregate GBP-hedged (LEGATRGH) Direct equivalent
SGLN Gold spot in GBP (XAU)                           No TER drag in proxy — slightly overstates return
ITPS US TIPS total return index (I01551US)            Direct equivalent
ICOM Bloomberg Commodity TR (BCOMTR)                  Direct equivalent
CSH2 SONIA rate (average of year-start and year-end)  Approximation — understates slightly in rising-rate years
IDTL ICE US Treasury 20+yr TR (LUATTRUU)              Direct equivalent

Target weights applied throughout: VWRP 30%, VAGS 18%, SGLN 15%, ITPS 14%, ICOM 10%, CSH2 7%, IDTL 6%. Annual rebalancing assumed. No transaction costs modelled. GBP base throughout. 2011 and 2016–2017 data gaps in the proxy set mean those years are excluded from the annual table — the cumulative figures bridge them.

What the Backtest Actually Proves

2008 — the machine's defining moment. Invictus −3.9% vs equity −18.8%. The 15pp gap is the entire case for the real-asset block. Gold +5.8% in GBP and IDTL +13.7% absorbed the equity collapse. A 60/40 still lost 8.2%. The machine lost less than half of that.

2013 — the machine's worst relative year. Gold crashed −28.3% in GBP. ICOM fell −11.3%. The real-asset block was simultaneously hit from two directions. Invictus returned +0.8% — positive, but 24pp behind equity. This is the honest cost of owning all four regimes. In a pure Goldilocks year with a simultaneous gold crash, the machine underperforms badly in relative terms.

2022 — the inflation shock validation. The 60/40 lost approximately 12%. Equity lost approximately 8% in GBP terms. Invictus was approximately flat, saved by ICOM +29.9%. This is the year the machine earned its architecture.

2025 — gold's GBP breakout. Gold rose 64.6% in GBP terms — the largest single-year GBP gold return in the dataset. SGLN at 15% weight contributed approximately 9.7pp to the pie. Invictus's best year in the backtest.

The honest cumulative verdict. Over 20 years, equity returned +420%. Invictus returned +186%. The gap is real, large, and the direct cost of owning the Stagflation and Deflation regimes during a period that was predominantly Goldilocks and Reflation. The machine did not lose money in 2008 when equity lost 19%. That insurance cost approximately 234pp of cumulative return over 20 years. Whether that insurance was worth it depends entirely on what the next 20 years look like.

The Honest Interpretation

What the backtest proves

The machine has a Sharpe ratio of 1.15 — higher than 100% equity (1.08) on a risk-adjusted basis. It delivered more return per unit of volatility than a pure equity portfolio, despite lower absolute returns.

The worst year was −3.9%. Equity's worst year was −18.8%. That 15pp difference in the worst year is the entire behavioural case for the machine — the investor who lost 3.9% in 2008 did not sell. Many who lost 18.8% did.

Invictus won outright in 3 of 15 years — 2007, 2008, and 2025. All three were years where the Reflation or Stagflation regime dominated. The machine wins when it is supposed to win.

What the Monte Carlo proves

At the median outcome, £200/month for 20 years produces £106,225 — more than double the £48,710 contributed. The machine more than doubles the contribution in the median case.

Even in the P5 worst case — the bottom 5% of all simulated outcomes — the portfolio reaches £76,194, still 56% above total contributions. The machine does not destroy capital in any realistic scenario when contributions are consistent.

The £100,000 target is achievable at the median — reached between Year 19 and Year 20 at £200/month. Increasing contributions to £300/month pulls that crossover to approximately Year 15.

What neither proves — and must be stated

The next 20 years will not look like the last 20. The last 20 years included the GFC, a decade of zero rates, a COVID shock, and an inflation surge. The next 20 may include a sovereign debt crisis, a dollar debasement cycle, a climate shock, or a prolonged Stagflation. The Monte Carlo uses historical volatility — it does not model tail events that have no historical precedent.

The 7.45% mean return is a backtest artefact. It includes 2025's gold +64.6% in GBP — an outlier that may not repeat. A more conservative forward assumption of 5.5–6.5% mean return would push the median 20-year outcome to approximately £85,000–£95,000 rather than £106,225.

The backtest does not include platform fees, spread costs, or tax drag outside the ISA. Inside the ISA, tax drag is zero. Platform fees at 0.15–0.25% per year reduce the effective return by approximately £150–£250 per year at £100,000 scale — material but not portfolio-altering.

The single variable that dominates all of this is contribution rate. The difference between £200/month and £400/month at the median outcome over 20 years is approximately £60,000 in final portfolio value. No asset allocation decision available to this portfolio produces a comparable impact.

The machine is validated by the data. It underperforms equity in bull markets by design, outperforms in crisis years by design, and delivers a higher Sharpe ratio than pure equity over the full cycle. The median Monte Carlo outcome doubles the money contributed. The worst realistic outcome still beats putting the money under a mattress by 56%. The rest is contribution rate and time.
